- [ ] CHARACTER ACTING, MICRO-EXPRESSIONS, PERSONALITY-DRIVEN PHYSICAL COMEDY (CB 2026-11-13 02:03 Bangkok, pasted as a rule): characters think, feel, anticipate and react; they never just perform a mechanical action. Every significant action carries ONE motivated human behaviour detail that comes from what the character is thinking or doing, never a random add-on: tongue slightly out of one mouth corner while concentrating, one eye squinted while aiming, biting the lower lip while working, one eyebrow up when confused, head tilt while thinking, holding the breath on a delicate task. Use facial asymmetry (brows and eyes move independently), eye direction and attention shifts, reaction delays, small wrist corrections and personality body language (tense shoulders, puffed chest). Write each gag as an ACTING SEQUENCE with times: how the behaviour starts, holds and changes, e.g. drilling 0.0-1.0 s studies the target, head tilt, eyes narrow; 1.0-2.0 leans in, tongue slowly comes out without him noticing; 2.0-3.5 drills, shoulders tense, tiny wrist corrections, brows furrowed; 3.5-4.2 drill jolts, he freezes, eyes wide, tongue still out; 4.2-5.0 slow glance to the camera, tongue back in, embarrassed. Comedy comes from tiny unconscious habits, awkward pauses, overconfidence and unexpected reactions (the nervous smile and pretend-nothing-happened after a mistake; the smug half-smile while pressing the wrong button). Faces sync with the action; no frozen faces, no repeated gestures, no random expressions, no robotic moves. HOW IT FITS OUR RULES: the face still starts calm and the big change lands ON the narration word (EXPRESSIONS); the behaviour detail plays DURING the action and the acting sequence; once a character has settled, DEAD STILL IN HOLDS still wins (blinks only, no idle breathing); the head wobble comes with each expression change. The writer marks the behaviour detail per action in the shot table, the animator acts it, and the character-artist adds any face part the rig lacks (tongue-out mouth, one-eye squint, independent brows, lip bite) as a request to the lead before the shot is built. - [ ] UNIVERSAL VISUAL COMEDY AND GAG SYSTEM (CB 2026-11-13 02:05 Bangkok, pasted as a rule; his text kept): Automatically identify opportunities for clever, context-aware visual humor throughout every animation scene. Do not wait for the script to explicitly request a gag. Invent original visual jokes that naturally emerge from the character's goals, personality, actions, props, environment, and current story situation. PRINCIPLES: 1 Situational comedy (jokes from what is happening in the scene, not random events). 2 Visual metaphors (poverty, exhaustion, confidence, stupidity, failure or success turned into clear funny physical images). 3 Prop comedy (ordinary objects get unexpected, exaggerated or absurd behaviour that reinforces the story). 4 Character-driven humor (unconscious habits, quirks, awkward gestures, overconfidence, hesitation, exaggerated reactions; see CHARACTER ACTING). 5 Expectation reversal (set up the obvious, reveal an unexpected but understandable outcome). 6 Escalation (a small problem grows into a bigger comic consequence). 7 Physical comedy (slapstick with believable body mechanics, balance, weight and timing; BODY MECHANICS, CONTACT AND GROUNDING still apply). 8 Background comedy (occasional subtle secondary jokes that reward attention without pulling focus from the main action). 9 Silent storytelling (the gag reads without narration, dialogue or captions). 10 Comedic timing (anticipation, setup, pause, reveal, reaction, recovery; a short stillness sells the punchline). CREATIVE FREEDOM: never repeat the same joke template; choose a different gag mechanism per scene; think like an experienced cartoon storyboard artist. INTEGRATION: only where the gag adds to the scene's meaning, pacing or personality; one strong readable gag beats several weak ones; the story stays coherent, characters stay consistent, essential narration and key information are never interrupted. EXECUTION: for each gag decide setup, anticipation, visual reveal, reaction and exit transition; sell it with micro-expressions, body language, props, secondary motion and camera framing, keeping IK, limb proportions, object contact, collision, ground placement and spatial continuity. QUALITY CONTROL before finalizing: is it understood at once, does it fit this exact scene, is it visually surprising, does it reveal character or reinforce the story, is the timing readable? If not, replace it or drop it. HOW WE RUN IT: the writer proposes one gag per beat in the shot table (mechanism named, so no two scenes reuse one); the camera-director frames the reveal; the animator and vfx-artist time it; the storyboard-judge runs the five quality questions; gags stay in the story's world and period and never copy a Rick gag.

- [ ] UNIVERSAL CHARACTER PERSONALITY, PSYCHOLOGY AND SOCIAL DYNAMICS (CB 2026-11-13 02:11 Bangkok, pasted as a rule; his 13 points kept): act as a character animation director, comedy storyboard artist and performance specialist. Every character has a distinct, persistent personality that shapes how they think, move, interact, react, solve problems and behave around others. 1 PERSONALITY PROFILE: temperament, confidence, intelligence, competence, fears, motivations, emotional habits, strengths, weaknesses, quirks; kept across scenes unless the story changes them. 2 PERSONALITY-DRIVEN MOVEMENT: a calm expert moves deliberately and efficiently; a nervous beginner hesitates and overcorrects; an arrogant one exaggerates gestures; a sceptic uses restrained reactions, crossed arms, doubtful glances. 3 SHOW, DON'T TELL: personality through action, face, posture, timing, eye contact, gestures, object handling and reactions, not narration. 4 COMPETENCE AND CONFIDENCE: real skill is not the same as shown confidence (the expert works casually and precisely; the overconfident amateur performs dramatically and makes obvious mistakes). 5 SOCIAL DYNAMICS AND STATUS: how characters see and treat each other (admiration, scepticism, rivalry, disrespect, friendship, jealousy, intimidation, shifting status), shown through glances, spacing, interruptions, body language and contrasting reactions. 6 CONTRAST COMEDY: calm expert among panicking onlookers, confident fool beside a worried genius, the underestimated one who succeeds. 7 HABITS AND MICRO-EXPRESSIONS matched to the individual (see CHARACTER ACTING). 8 REACTION CHOREOGRAPHY: supporting characters are directed as carefully as the lead: true eye lines, reaction delays, facial transitions, exchanged glances, group reactions, all based on what they actually see or know. 9 EMOTIONAL CONTINUITY: emotions develop through events; no random expression changes between shots; the state and the relationships hold until the story changes them. 10 PERSONALITY GAGS from traits, relationships, unexpected competence, overconfidence, misunderstandings and clashes (see GAG SYSTEM). 11 SUBTLETY AND TIMING: not every moment is big; contrast movement and stillness; a small eyebrow raise can beat a huge cartoon reaction. 12 CHARACTER CONSISTENCY: each character keeps their posture, movement rhythm, gesture vocabulary, facial habits, reaction speed and social behaviour through the whole film. 13 VISUAL STORYTELLING: changes in relationship and status are shown in performance (the dismissed one earns respect later and is still recognisably themselves). For every scene decide what each character wants, believes, feels about the others and knows, and how their personality shapes their behaviour. Never give all characters the same generic expressions, gestures or reactions. HOW WE RUN IT: the writer writes `episodes/<name>/CAST_PERSONALITY.md` before the shot table (one profile per speaking or reacting character: the 13-point profile in short, posture, movement rhythm, 3-5 signature gestures, reaction speed, relationship to each other character, and how it changes in the story); each shot table row says what each character in frame wants, knows and feels; the animator picks library moves and timing from the profile; reactions are actions, so once a character has reacted and settled, DEAD STILL IN HOLDS applies again; the storyboard-judge fails panels where two characters react the same generic way or a reaction does not match what that character saw.

- [ ] BODY MECHANICS, CONTACT AND GROUNDING (CB 2026-11-13 02:01 Bangkok: "aplly this rule"; his full list: anatomically plausible posing, contact-aware animation, IK, joint constraints, physically believable object interaction, no floating, no ground penetration). For our 2D vector puppets in the three.js kit and in Blender: (1) FIXED LIMB LENGTHS: arms and legs keep their rig length (the 19 px limb line) in every pose; never stretch a limb to reach. (2) IK, NOT LOOSE LIMBS: a hand or foot goes to a target point and the arm or leg is solved from the shoulder or hip with a two-bone bend at fixed lengths (ep_arms / grips.mjs for hands); if the target is out of reach, the body moves, turns or bends first (body-driven motion). (3) JOINT LIMITS: elbows and knees bend only the natural way, wrists and ankles within a natural range; no reversed knee, no broken elbow, no disconnected limb. (4) HANDS LOCKED ON OBJECTS: the centre of the round fist sits on the named grip point and stays locked there while held; the object follows the hand with no sliding, floating, drifting or clipping; reach, grip, lift, carry, set down, release are separate acted steps (approach with feet set, bend hips and knees, both hands on the grip points, lift with legs and body, carry stable, lower onto a surface before letting go). (5) WEIGHT: every move starts from the body with anticipation and follow-through; hips and feet shift when lifting, pushing, pulling or turning; the centre of gravity stays over the feet unless the character is falling; limbs move on arcs, no robotic straight interpolation and no needless limb motion. (6) FEET PLANTED: in a stance phase the foot's world position does not move (no foot sliding); walks use foot locking per step; standing characters have both weight-bearing feet on the ground. (7) GROUNDING: feet snap to the real ground height at their position (raycast to the terrain in three.js, the same positions in Blender from the lock); no hovering above, no sinking into floors, stairs, grass or furniture; a seated character touches the seat; every still prop rests on a support surface; unsupported things fall. (8) NO CLIPPING: bodies, limbs and props never pass through walls, furniture, other characters or each other (see PHYSICAL BODIES). (9) SPATIAL CONSISTENCY: scale, depth, height and world position stay consistent from frame to frame; no teleporting (NO TELEPORT). (10) FRAME CHECK BEFORE RENDER: before any render, check dense frames (every contact, every step, every grip and release, and every 0.5 s) for floating, ground penetration, clipping, foot sliding, missing support and broken contact; contact shadows (CONTACT SHADOW) go under feet and props, but they never hide a real gap. Cartoon exaggeration of anticipation and impact is welcome; contact points, ground placement and object hold stay physically right. CB's own wording of this rule: rules/CB_WORDING_ARCHIVE.md.
