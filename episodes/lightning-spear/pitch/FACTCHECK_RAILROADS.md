# FACT CHECK: railroads-kind pitches (checked 2026-11-15)

Budget: max 30 web calls. Results appended per pitch as checked.

## PITCH 1. Lightning rod tower (Italy ~1765)

Verdict: NEEDS WORDING (principle solid). 8 web calls used.

1. Franklin rod / almanack 1753: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Lightning rod: "conceived by Benjamin Franklin in 1749". Founders Online lists "Poor Richard Improved, 1753" (founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-04-02-0148); a search summary says the 1753 almanack carried rod instructions (not read in full). Safe wording: "Franklin, early 1750s".
2. Sound 340 m/s: NOT CHECKED (school physics, accepted).
3. 30,000 C: NOT CHECKED (commonly given by NOAA; accepted).
4. Bells rung against storms: CONFIRMED in practice (103 ringers died, see 5). "Fulgura frango": NOT CHECKED, no source found in 2 tries. Do not put it on screen as exact unless verified.
5. German count 386 towers / 103 ringers / 33 years: PARTLY. Wikipedia Bell-ringer: "In France between the years 1753 and 1786, 103 bell-ringers were killed during thunderstorms" (33 years, but FRANCE, not a German count). 386 towers and "Fischer, Munich 1784" not found. A different tally (Dray) says 121 killed 1750 to 1783. Safe wording: "about a hundred bell ringers died in French thunderstorms in 33 years".
6. Brescia 18 Aug 1769: PARTLY. Wikipedia Brescia explosion: lightning hit the San Nazaro bastion; "about 90,000 kg" of powder; "destroyed about one-sixth of the city". Deaths DISPUTED: official account two years later "400 dead and 800 wounded"; "3,000 often reported in later English sources"; up to 6,000 claimed. NB it was the Bastion of San Nazaro (Venetian powder store), not clearly "the church". Use "hundreds died" or keep it out.
7. St Mark's Campanile: PARTLY / WRONG on rod year. Wikipedia Campanile di San Marco: struck 7 June 1388, 11 Aug 1489, 1537, 1548, 1562, 1565, 1567, twice in 1582, 1653 (repair), 23 April 1745 (cracked, "killing four people in the square"), 1761 and 1762. Rod: "On 18 March 1776, Giuseppe Toaldo installed a lightning rod, the first in Venice." So the pitch's "1766" is WRONG (1776), and 1417 not found (1537, 1562, 1567, 1582, 1761, 1762 are extra). The page lists no strikes after 1776 (does not say so directly).
8. Rod is a safe path, does not attract more: CONFIRMED as accepted physics (not separately sourced; a rod makes the strike point predictable and conducts it down).

One line: can be told honestly as a fable on a true principle. Needed wording: Italy "about 1765" is fine for a fable but the Venice rod is 1776 and Brescia 1769 are after it, so say "in those years" or drop dates; keep gunpowder/deaths out (death toll disputed 400 to 3,000+); priest as kind mocker; real history had the Church objecting to rods then relenting, so avoid presenting the church as villain.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campanile_di_San_Marco , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brescia_explosion , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell-ringer , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lightning_rod , https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Franklin/01-04-02-0148

## PITCH 2. Bridge full of holes (Tacoma Narrows 1940)

Verdict: SOLID principle, NEEDS WORDING on cause. 6 web calls used.

1. Opened 1 July 1940, collapsed 7 Nov 1940: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940): "opened to traffic on July 1, 1940", collapsed November 7. WSDOT page: "barely four months after the bridge opened".
2. Wind about 40 mph: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia: main span fell in 40 mph (64 km/h) winds. ("42" not seen; not checked.)
3. Main span 2,800 ft, third longest: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia: 2,800 ft (853.4 m), "world's third-longest suspension bridge by main span".
4. 8 ft plate girders vs Eldridge 25 ft truss, Moisseiff: CONFIRMED. STRUCTURE magazine: Moisseiff "designed it with an 8-foot-deep plate girder serving in place of Eldridge's deep trusses"; Eldridge's design had "a 25-foot-deep stiffening truss on either side of the roadway". Note Moisseiff's firm was Moran & Proctor; Eldridge was a state engineer.
5. Galloping Gertie, people drove to ride: CONFIRMED. WSDOT: a "boomer" "dubbed the bouncing span, Galloping Gertie". HistoryLink: drivers "wait until the wind was right, drive up to Tacoma Narrows, then wait in line to ride the bridge".
6. Nobody died, dog Tubby lost: CONFIRMED. HistoryLink: "Tubby was the only fatality." Wikipedia: a Cocker Spaniel named Tubby; no human deaths reported. (Tubby was not rescued; photographer turned back. Sensitive: keep the dog out or show him escaping only if marked as fable.)
7. 1950 bridge truss 33 ft deep: PARTLY / NOT CONFIRMED. A search snippet said "33-foot truss" (snippet cut off); no opened page confirmed it. Wikipedia: 1950 replacement opened 14 Oct 1950, deeper truss, no depth given. Say "a deep open truss".
8. Cause = aeroelastic flutter, not simple resonance: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia: caused by aeroelastic flutter, not resonance, as textbooks said. Safe child's line: "wind kept pushing the twist bigger"; "holes let the wind walk through" is a simplification (real fix was deep open truss plus slots/deflectors and stiffness; it is the open truss and deeper section, not holes cut later).
9. Deck 28 ft higher one side: NOT CHECKED / not found in 3 sources (Wikipedia says "several feet" in mild winds). Do not use the number on screen.

One line: can be told as a fable on a true principle. Needed wording: say "a thin solid-sided bridge twisted in a wind of about 40 mph (64 km/h)"; the hero's "holes" are really an open deep truss built that way from the start (not cut afterwards), and he is Eldridge-like; no real names; Tubby sensitive; no human deaths so it is gentle. Famous event, so tell it as the fable that explains why.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1940) , https://www.structuremag.org/article/tacoma-narrows-bridge-failure-1940/ , https://historylink.org/file/5048 , https://wsdot.wa.gov/TNBHistory/stories-1940-bridge.htm


## PITCH 3. Dam with a notch (Johnstown 1889)

Verdict: SOLID facts, NEEDS WORDING (real tragedy). 7 web calls used (3 wasted on 404/redirects).

1. 31 May 1889, 2,209 dead: CONFIRMED with a note. Wikipedia Johnstown Flood: "calculated originally as 2,209 people", official toll later given as 2,208 (Wikipedia South Fork Dam says 2,208). Say "more than 2,200". NPS pages opened (south-fork-dam page, one 404) gave no numbers.
2. Earth dam 72 ft high, 931 ft long: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia South Fork Dam: "72 feet (22 m) high and 931 feet (284 m) long". Pitch said "about 900", fine; 931 is the exact figure.
3. Fish screens clogged: PARTLY. Wikipedia Johnstown Flood: "an iron grate and a broken fish trap had become obstructed with debris from the swollen waterline" (spillway obstructed; wording is grate/fish trap, not "screens" exactly).
4. Top lowered 2 to 3 ft, pipes removed: CONFIRMED, it is 3 ft. Wikipedia: "Workers lowered the dam, which had been 72 feet high, by 3 feet" to carry a carriageway road. Pipes: Wikipedia South Fork Dam: a previous owner "had removed and sold for scrap the five cast iron discharge pipes". Fable should say "about 3 feet", not 2.
5. Failed in under an hour, 20 million tons: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia: breach "between 2:50 and 2:55 pm"; witnesses said the lake emptied in "35-45 minutes". "20 million tons of water" is in Wikipedia South Fork Dam; Johnstown Flood page gives 14.55 million m3 (3.843 billion gallons).
6. Principle (overtopped earth dam fails, spillway needed): NOT CHECKED with a source (the engineering case-study page 404'd). Accepted dam engineering; Wikipedia says the lake was "nearly cresting the dam" on the morning of 31 May. Do not state a number on screen for this.
7. 6 to 10 inches of rain in 24 hours: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Johnstown Flood: "6 to 10 inches (150 to 250 mm) of rain fell in 24 hours over the region".

One line: can be told as a fable on a true principle, if set in an invented place (pitch's Scotland 1885 is fine). Needed wording: say "water over the top" not "flood killed"; village warned and safe; never imply the real victims; do not name South Fork or Johnstown on screen unless a respectful dedication; in the fable the cause is a man-made flaw (blocked notch, lowered top), which is true to the real event. Note the real failure was also due to missing pipes and poor maintenance, not just a blocked spillway.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Fork_Dam , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnstown_Flood


## PITCH 4. Mortarless Inca wall (Cusco 1950)

Verdict: NEEDS WORDING (key "dancing stones" claim is unproven). 4 web calls used (2 fetches + 2 searches; search summaries, not full pages, for the 1950 date and the study).

1. Mortarless, knife-tight joints: PARTLY. Wikipedia Inca architecture: "closely fitted without mortar" (no knife-blade wording there; Protzen not opened). Wikipedia Coricancha quotes chronicler Cieza de Leon: "no mortar, either of earth or lime, but a sort of bitumen" so some chroniclers describe a binding; safe wording: "stones cut to fit with almost no gap".
2. Inward lean, trapezoid doors: CONFIRMED qualitatively. Wikipedia Inca architecture: walls "slightly inclined inside"; "doors, niches and windows, usually had a trapezoidal shape". Angle: NOT CONFIRMED. No field-measured degrees found; one unsourced blog says 5 to 10. Do not state "5 degrees" as fact; say "leaning a little inward".
3. Cusco quake 21 May 1950, Santo Domingo damaged, Inca walls stood: CONFIRMED with correction. Search summary of sources (Santo Domingo Wikipedia page, WCEE2012 paper, a photo caption) gives 21 May 1950; the church was badly damaged (tower arches collapsed, apse wall cracked, large part of convent destroyed), NOT wholly collapsed; Inca masonry "not affected"/only a handful of fractures. Wikipedia Coricancha: "Major earthquakes severely damaged the church... the Inca stone walls... still stand." 1650: the convent collapsed (Wikipedia Coricancha: "built after the 1650 earthquake destroyed the first Dominican convent") and Inca foundations barely moved. Pitch wording "broke" is OK; "fell flat" would be wrong.
4. "Stones dance and settle back": LEGEND (unmeasured). Only non-academic sources; the peer-reviewed item found (Ogawa, Cuadra, Karkee, Rojas 2004, rigid-body model, WIT Press) says a uniform friction between blocks lets the wall act as one rigid body and that Inca stone structures survived quakes. Wikipedia: "During a small or moderate earthquake, masonry was stable"; also "peerless seismic resistance". Wording must be "people say the stones dance", never shown as measured. Also, some Inca walls at Sacsayhuaman, Chinchero, Puka Pukara show earthquake damage, so "never fall" is false.
5. Hammerstones, no iron: PARTLY CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Inca architecture: "It is speculated that stones were initially fit using hammerstones" and hammerstone marks are visible. (Protzen experiments not opened.)
6. Twelve-angled stone: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia: "Twelve-angled stone in the Hatun Rumiyoc street of Cusco" (Inca masonry example).
7. Sacsayhuaman stones over 100 t: NOT CHECKED.
8. Clay mortar in common walls: PARTLY. Wikipedia: "adobe walls were also quite common, usually laid over stone foundations"; "dirt set in mortar" mentioned; so an adobe rival is fair.

One line: can be told as a fable on a true principle if the "dance" is hedged ("they say") and no degrees/measured claim is shown; keep Spanish out (church in real event is Spanish, which the fable already drops); no Inca religion shown mockingly (Coricancha was the Sun temple, sacred); the "tight, leaning, trapezoid, no strong glue" principle is sound, the claim that wall survives while a rigid one fails in the 1950 quake is true as comparison of Inca wall vs Spanish church.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_architecture , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coricancha , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_and_Convent_of_Santo_Domingo,_Cusco (search result only) , https://cris.pucp.edu.pe/es/publications/evaluation-of-the-seismic-behaviour-of-an-inca-stone-wall-using-r-2/ (search result only)


## PITCH 5. House on legs (Yakutsk)

Verdict: NEEDS WORDING (principle true; period claim OPEN). 4 web calls used (total 30 for all five).

1. Yakutsk largest city on continuous permafrost, houses on piles: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Yakutsk: "Yakutsk is the largest city built on continuous permafrost" and "many houses there are built on concrete piles" (concrete, i.e. modern). Wikipedia Permafrost: the Melnikov Permafrost Institute (Yakutsk) found piles "should extend down to 15 metres (49 ft)". "Air gap" not stated in either; it is the standard reason but not sourced here.
2. January average about -39 C: PARTLY. Wikipedia Yakutsk gives "-36.9 C in January" (average monthly). Say "about minus 37" or "nearly minus 40". Also "coldest town on earth" not Yakutsk (the pitch already notes this); say "one of the coldest cities".
3. Shergin shaft 1828 to 1837, 116 m, never left frozen ground: CONFIRMED (search summaries: Alaska Science Forum "Schergin's Well", Encyclopedia Arctica, an AWI poster). Dug from 1828 (one source says 1827) to 1837; about 116.4 m (sources 116 to 116.6 m; Alaska Science Forum: 382 ft = 116 m); Shergin never found water, ground frozen all the way; bottom about -0.6 C. Use "about 116 metres".
4. Active layer 1 to 3 m: PARTLY. Wikipedia Permafrost confirms an active layer that "freezes and thaws annually" but gave no thickness; search on Dawson says active layer up to 5 m there. Say "the top metre or two".
5. Heated buildings melt permafrost and sink or tilt: CONFIRMED. Wikipedia Permafrost: "the heat of the building ... can spread to the soil, thawing it", and the building "is destabilized"; example of a 1901 Trans-Siberian steam engine factory that "began to crumble within a month of operations". Dawson City: sources (search summary: Yukon Geological Survey, CBC, Grist) describe old buildings leaning, heat from buildings contributing to thaw; much of the Dawson tilting is also from climate warming (more than 2.4 C since 1901). "Kissing Buildings" name NOT CHECKED.
6. Trans-Alaska Pipeline about 78,000 supports: NOT CHECKED. Wikipedia Permafrost mentions heat pipes in vertical supports but gives no count. Leave it out.
7. Siberian houses on legs about 1900: NOT CHECKED, remains OPEN. No source found; the only 1901 example found is a failing factory on permafrost (shows builders did not always know). Keep the date vague ("long ago") or say "an old trick", and do not claim the method was standard before Soviet times.

One line: can be told as a fable on a true principle ("a warm house on frozen ground sinks; lift it so the frost stays"), with the date vague and the town unnamed or "one of the coldest cities"; sinking is slow, no deaths, no people shown badly (invent the merchant; do not tie to Sakha people or any ethnic group); soft and funny ending as the writer says.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakutsk , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permafrost , https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/schergins-well (search result only) , https://grist.org/climate-energy/in-the-yukon-climate-change-is-making-buildings-fall-down/ (search result only)
