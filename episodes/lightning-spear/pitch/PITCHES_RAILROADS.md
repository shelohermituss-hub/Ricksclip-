# PITCHES, ROUND 2: the railroads kind (writer, 2026-11-15)

The kind: a laughed-at hero does something to a big built thing that looks like sabotage. A rival builds it "perfect" and wins first. Nature arrives in small countable steps. The perfect one fails. The flawed one stands.

All five are fables: unnamed hero, invented boss and rival. Each rests on a real principle and a real event.
All fact marks are from the writer's memory. No web page or book was opened. The lead must check them before CB picks.

## PITCH 1. Why The Safest Tower Had A Spear On Top

- Fable set in: a hill town in northern Italy, about 1765. Two rival church towers on two hills.
- Real behind it: Franklin's lightning rod (1750s). The powder blast of Brescia, 1769. The bell tower of St Mark's in Venice, hit again and again until it got a rod.

THE STRANGE ACT (frame 0): He nailed an iron spear to the top of the church tower, in a town that was terrified of lightning.

THE TWIST: Lightning always takes the easiest road to the ground. His spear and one iron wire as thick as a thumb ARE the easy road. The bolt runs down the wire into the earth and the tower is not touched.

THE LAST PICTURE: Night storm. The hero stands calm in front of his tower with the spear glowing. Behind him on the other hill the perfect tower burns like a candle with its roof gone.

- The mocker: the fat town priest who owns the tower. "You are inviting the lightning into my church!" He orders the bells rung against every storm.
- The perfect rival: the builder of the other tower. Taller, white marble, a gold angel on top, not one piece of iron outside. He has the biggest storm bell in the land.
- Nature arrives in 3 steps (the town counts the seconds between flash and thunder):
  1. Spring storm. Flash, then ten seconds, then thunder. Far away. Everybody laughs at the spear.
  2. Summer storm. Five seconds. A tree on the ridge splits. The rival's bell ringer drops the rope and runs.
  3. Autumn storm. One second. Two bolts. One runs down the hero's wire with a blue flash and a puff of steam in the mud. One hits the gold angel and the marble tower cracks and burns.

Places and colour worlds:
1. Tower top above the roofs, dusk before a storm. Terracotta orange roofs, purple cloud wall, one iron spear.
2. Town square on market day, noon sun. Yellow walls, striped awnings in red and white, green shutters.
3. Inside the church, candle light. Gold altar, deep red cloth, blue painted ceiling with stars.
4. The rival's hill, bright morning. White marble tower, gold angel, cypress trees dark green.
5. The bell chamber, storm light. Bronze bell, rope, the ringer lit white by flashes.
6. Vineyard ridge between the hills, summer storm. Green vines, grey rain, a split burning tree.
7. Very close: the foot of the wire going into wet earth. Blue spark, steam, one scared chicken.
8. Final night storm over both hills. Deep blue sky, white bolts, orange fire.
Light kinds: golden noon, candle interior, purple dusk, blue night storm with flashes.

Facts to check:
1. Franklin proposed the pointed rod about 1750 and described it in Poor Richard's Almanack for 1753. SOLID. Source: Franklin's letters and the almanack.
2. Sound travels about 340 metres a second, so three seconds between flash and thunder is about one kilometre. SOLID. School physics.
3. A lightning bolt is about 30,000 degrees C, hotter than the surface of the sun. SOLID. NOAA and physics texts.
4. Church bells were rung against storms and many carried the words "Fulgura frango" (I break the lightning). SOLID. Histories of bells.
5. A German count: in 33 years lightning hit 386 church towers and killed 103 bell ringers. LIKELY. Quoted from Fischer, Munich 1784. I have only seen it quoted second hand.
6. Brescia, 18 August 1769: lightning hit the church of San Nazaro where about 90 tonnes of gunpowder were stored. About 3,000 died and a sixth of the city fell. LIKELY. Numbers vary by source. Check.
7. The bell tower of St Mark's in Venice was hit and damaged many times (1388, 1417, 1489, 1548, 1565, 1653, 1745 and more). It got a rod in 1766 and had no lightning damage after. LIKELY. Check the years.
8. A rod does not pull more lightning in. It gives the bolt a safe road of metal into the ground. SOLID. Any physics text.

Risk: church and priest as the mocker need a light, kind touch. Keep the gunpowder out of the fable or the ending turns into a disaster.

## PITCH 2. Why The Best Bridge Was Full Of Holes

- Fable set in: the cold north-west coast of America, about 1940. Two towns, two bridges over two sea straits.
- Real behind it: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge, "Galloping Gertie". It opened on 1 July 1940 and tore itself apart on 7 November 1940.

THE STRANGE ACT (frame 0): He cut holes into the sides of his own brand new bridge, hundreds of them, until you could see the sea through it.

THE TWIST: A bridge with solid smooth sides catches the wind like a sail. A wind of only 40 miles an hour made the real one twist until it broke. Holes let the wind walk through.

THE LAST PICTURE: Grey storm morning. The hero leans on the rail of his ugly cage bridge with his hat blown off. Behind him the last piece of the slim bridge hangs from its cables over the white water.

- The mocker: the bridge company boss in a fur collar coat. "I paid for a bridge, not a bird cage!" The newspaper prints a cartoon of it as Swiss cheese.
- The perfect rival: a famous designer with a silver pencil. His bridge is a thin smooth ribbon, the most elegant in the world. All the cars and all the photographers go there.
- Nature arrives in 3 steps:
  1. Light breeze. A worker's coffee cup slides along the rail of the smooth bridge. People giggle.
  2. Stronger wind. The deck rolls in waves. Cars ahead sink out of sight and pop up again. People pay to ride it for fun.
  3. Forty miles an hour. The deck twists like a wrung towel, lamp posts tilt left and right, and the middle drops into the strait. On the hero's bridge only his hat moves.

Places and colour worlds:
1. High on the steel side of the bridge, clear cold sunrise. Orange primer steel, torch sparks, teal water far below.
2. The strait seen wide, summer noon. Deep blue water, dark green fir forest, two bridges far apart.
3. The boss's office, lamp light. Brown wood, green glass lamp, a brass model of the bridge.
4. Opening day on the slim bridge. Silver grey deck, red ribbon, flags and bunting in red, white and blue.
5. Newspaper press room. Black ink, yellow light, white paper rolls.
6. The rolling deck in autumn fog. Soft grey, yellow headlights, cars vanishing in the dips.
7. Very close: one cut hole with wind whistling through it and a gull flying through.
8. Storm morning from the water. Slate sky, white foam, falling grey steel.
Light kinds: pink sunrise, bright summer noon, warm lamp interior, autumn fog, storm grey.

Facts to check:
1. Opened 1 July 1940, collapsed 7 November 1940. SOLID. Washington State Department of Transportation history pages.
2. Wind at the collapse was about 40 miles an hour (often given as 42). SOLID. Same source and the 1941 federal report.
3. The main span was 2,800 feet, third longest suspension span in the world then. SOLID.
4. The sides were solid plate girders only 8 feet deep. The first design by Clark Eldridge had an open truss 25 feet deep. The slim design was by Leon Moisseiff. SOLID.
5. Workers called it Galloping Gertie and people drove there to ride the waves. SOLID.
6. Nobody died. One dog, Tubby, was lost in a car. SOLID.
7. The 1950 bridge on the same spot has an open truss 33 feet deep and open grates in the deck. It still stands. SOLID. Check "33 feet".
8. The cause was twisting flutter fed by the wind, not simple "resonance" as old school books say. SOLID. Billah and Scanlan, American Journal of Physics 1991. Our line "the wind walks through the holes" is a child's version and must stay honest.
9. During the twist one edge of the deck was 28 feet higher than the other. LIKELY. From the eyewitness reports. Check.

Risk: the real event is famous. The fresh part is the hero who cuts holes on purpose. Real open trusses are built open, not cut later.

## PITCH 3. Why The Best Dam Had A Piece Missing

- Fable set in: the Scottish Highlands, about 1885. Two lairds, two glens, two earth dams.
- Real behind it: the South Fork Dam above Johnstown, Pennsylvania. It broke on 31 May 1889.

THE STRANGE ACT (frame 0): He dug a wide notch into the top of his own finished dam, so the lake could never be full.

THE TWIST: An earth dam dies the moment water runs over its top, because running water eats earth. The notch (a spillway) lets extra water out on a floor of stone. The real dam had its spillway blocked with fish screens and its top lowered by about 2 feet.

THE LAST PICTURE: Dawn after the rain. The hero sits on his dam beside the notch, which roars like a waterfall. Across the ridge the other glen is a brown empty bowl with the rival's boats lying in the mud.

- The mocker: the laird who paid for the lake. "I ordered a full lake! You gave me a leaking bathtub!" His lake stands 3 feet lower than the rival's.
- The perfect rival: a proud dam builder for the laird next door. Smooth grass top, level as a table, a carriage road along it, and iron screens across the outlet so not one of his prize trout escapes.
- Nature arrives in 3 steps (a white measuring post in each lake):
  1. One night of rain. The rival's water is 3 feet under the top. Trout jump. He toasts with the lairds.
  2. Two days of rain. Leaves and branches clog the fish screens. The water is 1 foot under the top. The hero's notch starts to run.
  3. Third night. On the perfect dam a film of water as thin as a finger runs over the grass. It cuts a groove, then a gap, then the whole lake leaves.

Places and colour worlds:
1. Top of the hero's dam, bright spring day. Fresh brown earth, green glen, blue lake, a spade.
2. The laird's castle hall, firelight. Red tartan, stag heads, dark oak.
3. The rival's lake party, summer afternoon. Mirror blue water, white sailing boats, purple heather hills.
4. Very close: the fish screen with silver trout behind it, then leaves and twigs piling on it.
5. The glen in three days of rain. Grey green, mist, swollen brown streams.
6. Lantern night on the perfect dam. Black blue rain, yellow lantern, the thin shine of water on the grass.
7. The village under the dam, wet dawn. White cottages, a red post box, people on the church hill.
8. The empty bowl at sunrise. Brown mud, pink sky, boats on their sides.
Light kinds: spring sun, firelight, summer gold, three day rain, lantern night, pink dawn.

Facts to check:
1. The South Fork Dam failed on 31 May 1889 and 2,209 people died in and around Johnstown. SOLID. Johnstown Flood National Memorial (US National Park Service). David McCullough, The Johnstown Flood.
2. It was an earth dam about 72 feet high and about 900 feet long. SOLID. Check both numbers.
3. The owners, the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club, put fish screens across the spillway. They clogged with debris in the storm. SOLID.
4. The top of the dam had been lowered (about 2 to 3 feet) to make a road, and the outlet pipes had been removed years before. SOLID. Check the feet.
5. The dam was overtopped and then washed out in under an hour. About 20 million tons of water left the lake. SOLID. Check the number.
6. Principle: an earth dam must never be overtopped. A spillway is the planned low place lined with rock or stone. SOLID. Any dam engineering text.
7. Six to ten inches of rain fell in 24 hours before the break. LIKELY. Check.

Risk: the real event killed over two thousand people. The fable must empty the rival's lake into an empty glen with the village warned and safe.

## PITCH 4. Why The King's Best Wall Had No Glue

- Fable set in: the Inca capital in the Andes, about 1450. Two masons each build one wall of the king's new sun temple.
- Real behind it: the Coricancha walls in Cusco, Peru. In the earthquake of 21 May 1950 the Spanish church built on top broke. The Inca walls under it stood.

THE STRANGE ACT (frame 0): He kicked the king's bucket of mortar off the mountain and stacked the temple wall from loose stones, leaning inward like a tired man.

THE TWIST: A wall glued stiff cracks when the ground shakes. Loose stones cut to fit so tight that a knife blade will not go in can hop, dance and sit back down. The lean of about 5 degrees makes them fall inward onto each other.

THE LAST PICTURE: Golden morning dust. The hero leans on his crooked wall, cheek on the stone, and pats it. Behind him the straight wall is a pile of bricks with one llama on top.

- The mocker: the king's chief builder with a gold ear plug and a knotted counting cord. "A wall with no glue is a pile!" The hero cuts one stone a week.
- The perfect rival: a fast mason with square clay bricks and thick white mortar. His wall is ruler straight, painted and finished in one season. He gets the feather cloak.
- Nature arrives in 3 steps:
  1. A small tremor. A hairline crack runs up the rival's paint. On the hero's wall one stone hops and drops back. A coca leaf placed in a joint is still held tight.
  2. A stronger shake at the harvest feast. Mortar crumbs rain on the rival's side. The crack is now wide enough for a finger. The hero's stones chatter like teeth and close again.
  3. The big one at dawn. The straight wall leans out and falls flat. The hero's wall dances, every stone in the air for a blink, and lands in place.

Places and colour worlds:
1. Mountain quarry edge above the clouds, cold blue dawn. Grey granite, white cloud sea, a falling bucket.
2. The temple yard, high noon. Gold plates on the walls, red and yellow striped cloth, blinding sun.
3. Green farm terraces like giant stairs, rainy season. Emerald steps, mist, llamas with red ear tassels.
4. Very close: the joint between two stones. A bronze knife cannot enter. A coca leaf is pinched in it.
5. The king's hall at night. Torch orange, feather cloaks in turquoise and pink, a gold sun disc.
6. Harvest feast on the plaza, dry season sunset. Purple maize, orange sky, dancers.
7. A rope bridge over a deep blue gorge, where stones are carried across.
8. The morning after, golden dust in the air.
Light kinds: cold blue dawn, white high noon, green rain mist, torch night, orange sunset, gold dust morning.

Facts to check:
1. The best Inca walls use no mortar and the joints are too tight for a knife blade. SOLID. Protzen, Inca Architecture and Construction at Ollantaytambo (1993).
2. Inca walls lean inward and doors and niches are trapezoids. SOLID. Same source. The angle "about 5 degrees" is LIKELY. Check (3 to 5 is often given).
3. Cusco earthquake of 21 May 1950: the church and convent of Santo Domingo were badly damaged and the Inca walls of the Coricancha beneath stood. SOLID. Check the date. The same happened in the 1650 earthquake. LIKELY.
4. "The stones dance and settle back in place." LIKELY. Said by many engineers and guides. I know no measurement of it. Word it as "people say".
5. The stones were shaped with harder river stones as hammers, not iron tools. SOLID. Protzen's experiments.
6. The famous twelve-angled stone in Cusco. SOLID. Good for the close look.
7. The biggest stones at Sacsayhuaman weigh over 100 tonnes. LIKELY. Estimates vary.
8. The Inca did use clay mortar for common walls, so a mortar rival is fair. LIKELY.

Risk: "dancing stones" is the heart of it and is the least proven claim. Also keep the Spanish out of the fable. It is Inca against Inca.

## PITCH 5. Why The Warmest House Stood In The Air

- Fable set in: a fur and tea trading town in eastern Siberia, about 1900.
- Real behind it: Yakutsk, the biggest city built on ground that never thaws. Its houses stand on piles. Dawson City in Canada has old houses that sank crooked.

THE STRANGE ACT (frame 0): In the coldest town on earth he sawed the floor of his new house free and jacked the whole house one metre up into the icy wind, onto wooden legs.

THE TWIST: The ground there is mud frozen hard as rock. A warm house sitting on it melts it, and the house sinks into its own puddle. An air gap of one metre lets the frost blow under the floor, so the ground stays frozen and holds.

THE LAST PICTURE: Bright summer evening. The hero drinks tea on his level porch on stilts. Next door the grand house is sunk to the window sills, tilted like a ship, and its owner climbs out of the roof window.

- The mocker: the rich merchant who ordered the house, in a huge fur coat. "Wind under my floor? At fifty below? My feet will freeze off!"
- The perfect rival: the town's master carpenter. He builds the neighbour's house flat on the ground on a thick stone footing, with three stoves and double floors. It is the warmest, finest house in Siberia. The whole town visits to get warm.
- Nature arrives in 3 steps (it is the house's own heat plus the summer):
  1. Late winter. In the warm house a dropped marble rolls slowly to one corner. Nobody cares.
  2. Spring. The front door will not close. A soup bowl slides down the table. The stove pipe leans. A puddle shines under the wall.
  3. Summer. One corner drops with a slurp. The house tips, the samovar rolls out of the door, and the porch sinks to the rail. Under the hero's house a husky sleeps on hard dry ground.

Places and colour worlds:
1. The building lot at winter noon, minus 50. White snow, pale pink low sun, breath clouds, a house on jacks.
2. Inside the warm rival house. Honey wood, a blue and white tiled stove, a brass samovar, red carpets.
3. The frozen river market. Frozen fish standing upright like firewood, fur traders, sled dogs, ice blocks in turquoise.
4. Night street under the northern lights. Green and violet sky, blue snow, yellow windows with carved frames.
5. Very close: under the hero's floor. Frost crystals on the legs, hard grey ground, the wind blowing through.
6. Spring thaw street. Brown mud, plank sidewalks, dripping icicles, bright blue sky.
7. Summer taiga edge at the white night. Bright green larch, purple fireweed, clouds of mosquitoes, sun at midnight.
8. The sunk house in the golden evening.
Light kinds: pink polar noon, lamp interior, northern lights night, wet spring sun, golden white night.

Facts to check:
1. Yakutsk is the largest city built on continuous permafrost and most of its buildings stand on piles with an air gap. SOLID. Encyclopedia entries and many reports.
2. The January average in Yakutsk is about minus 39 degrees C. SOLID. Climate tables. "Coldest town on earth" belongs to Oymyakon or Verkhoyansk. Word it with care.
3. Shergin's shaft in Yakutsk was dug from 1828 to 1837, reached 116 metres and never left frozen ground. LIKELY. Check the numbers.
4. Only the top 1 to 3 metres thaw each summer (the active layer). SOLID. Permafrost textbooks.
5. Heated buildings set straight on ice-rich permafrost melt it and sink or tilt. SOLID. Dawson City has leaning gold rush buildings from this. LIKELY for the named "Kissing Buildings". Check.
6. The Trans-Alaska Pipeline (1977) is held above the ground on about 78,000 supports for the same reason. LIKELY. Check the number. Not needed in the fable.
7. Were houses in Siberia about 1900 already built on legs? LEGEND? I am not sure. Old log houses there often simply sank. The pile method became the rule in Soviet times. The fable's date may have to move or stay vague.

Risk: the failure is slow and soft (a sinking house, no crash), so the ending is funny, not spectacular. Fact 7 is open.

## Writer's ranking
1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

Why: pitch 1 has the strongest frame 0 (a man nailing a spear onto a tower under a storm cloud), the countdown of seconds is a built-in clock, and the physics is school solid. Pitch 2 has the best documented event and the biggest collapse, but the event is well known. Pitch 3 has the clearest countable steps (3 feet, 1 foot, over) but sits on a real tragedy. Pitch 4 has the richest colours but its key claim is only LIKELY. Pitch 5 is the funniest and the softest ending, with one open fact.

Difference check: Italy 1765 lightning tower. USA 1940 wind bridge. Scotland 1885 flood dam. Peru 1450 earthquake wall. Siberia 1900 thaw house.
