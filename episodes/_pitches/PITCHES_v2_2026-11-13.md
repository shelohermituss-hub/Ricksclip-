## PITCH 1: "Why Two Farmers Raked the Moon"
Kind: smuggling, hide in plain sight + play dumb. Era/place: the old smuggling days (1700s), an English chalk-downs village with a duck pond, told as a fable.

**Draft (229 words, present tense from sentence 2):**
He tied a rock to a barrel of brandy and rolled it into the village duck pond. His buddy watches it glug to the bottom. "So, we're getting the ducks drunk." You see, it's called sinking the tubs. Brandy tax is sky high, so the whole village put every penny into twelve smuggled barrels, and the taxman rides through tonight. So all twelve sink on one rope, tied to a wooden duck. At midnight, they wade in with hay rakes to drag them out, and the taxman gallops up. His horse stops dead, and he flies over its head into the reeds. But his young helper isn't stupid. He holds up the lantern and counts the ducks. Eleven are asleep with their heads tucked in, and one is wide awake, and it hasn't blinked all night. So the farmer points at the moon in the water. "We're raking out that big round cheese." His buddy doesn't even blink. "Very big cheese." And the taxman laughs so hard he nearly falls back in. "They can't tell the moon from a cheese, boy. You think they could carve a duck?" Next morning at the inn, he tells everyone about the two fools who tried to rake the moon. He laughs so hard he buys the whole room a round, poured from a barrel with a wooden duck still tied to it.

**Twist structure**
- Act: a farmer sinks a brandy barrel, a rock lashed to it, in the duck pond (frame 0: barrel mid-roll, splash, scattering ducks, buddy deadpan).
- Trick explained: "sinking the tubs": twelve barrels on one rope, marked by a wooden decoy duck.
- Threat: the taxman's sharp young helper counts ducks; the one that never blinks is the tell.
- Saved by: the taxman's contempt: "They can't tell the moon from a cheese. You think they could carve a duck?"
- Reversal: the taxman pays for a round of the very brandy he rode out to seize (the village gets paid by the law). Last words "a wooden duck still tied to it" loop to frame 0 (barrel, rope, pond).

**Hero rig: the tub line** (one line, one picture per step): 1. twelve small oak casks come off a cart, slung in pairs on rope; 2. a flint sinker-stone is lashed to each (MACRO: the knot, brandy seeping at the bung); 3. all twelve are tied along one long rope; 4. the farmer paints the decoy duck, giving it WIDE-OPEN eyes (the plant for the tell), and ties it to the rope end; 5. barrels rolled in, glug, bubbles, ducks flee; 6. camera inside the hero object: from the pond floor, looking up past the barrels at the moon wobbling on the surface as rake teeth come down; 7. result: the dripping barrel on the inn counter, pond weed and duck still attached.
**Places (one village):** the duck pond with reeds and a willow; the lane with the inn sign; the inn's taproom; the church tower and a chalk hill figure behind everything for orientation.
**Lights:** pink-gold SUNSET (the sinking, frame 0), blue MOONLIGHT with one warm lantern pool (the raid, the tell, the save), warm HEARTH + morning window light at the inn (the payoff).
**Cast (gag each):** the Farmer (schemer, standard; calm, sly, rake over shoulder): points at the moon with total sincerity. The Buddy (thin; straw in mouth, never blinks): stands waist-deep holding the rake like a fishing rod. The Taxman (fat; tricorne, big boots, brass-buttoned coat): comes up out of the reeds with a real duck sitting on his hat. The Young Helper (standard; lantern, notebook): counts ducks on his fingers, runs out of fingers at eleven.
**Real vs legend:** the Moonrakers legend (Wiltshire villagers caught raking a pond for hidden smuggled brandy said they were raking out "the cheese", the moon's reflection, and the excisemen rode off laughing at the yokels) HIGH as a legend, LOW as an event; several villages claim it (Bishops Cannings, All Cannings, Devizes' Crammer pond). Smugglers' "tubs" sunk on weighted lines and later dragged up ("creeping") HIGH, mostly on the coast. High duty on brandy, 1700s HIGH. The word "moonrakers" for Wiltshire people in print by the late 1700s: MEDIUM. Twelve barrels, the decoy duck, the helper, the blink tell and the inn round: DRAMATIZED. Sources: Wiltshire Museum (Devizes); Ralph Whitlock, *The Folklore of Wiltshire* (1976); Kathleen Wiltshire, *Wiltshire Folklore* (1975); Francis Grose, *A Provincial Glossary* (1787); Richard Platt, *Smuggling in the British Isles* (2007); Mary Waugh, *Smuggling in Kent and Sussex 1700-1840* (1985); HM Customs and Excise collection, Merseyside Maritime Museum.

## PITCH 2: "The Chess Robot Nobody Could Beat"
Kind: machine hoax with a hidden operator. Era/place: a European imperial palace (built 1770, the emperor's game 1809), told as a fable.

**Draft (228 words):**
He folded a grown man into a wooden cabinet and set a wooden robot on top. His buddy's knees are up by his ears. "So, I'm the robot." You see, it's called a chess machine. Anyone who plays it loses, because the best chess player in town is hiding inside. He promised the emperor a wonder, spent six months and every last coin on it, and the emperor comes tomorrow. Inside, there's a sliding seat, a candle and a little board, and every piece up top has a magnet that tugs a button underneath. Next morning, the emperor sweeps in, steps on his own cape and sits down hard on the floor. But he's brought his scientist, and the scientist trusts nothing. He opens every little door with a candle. Gears, gears and more gears. But then he sniffs. A thin curl of smoke is rising out of the robot's hat. Machines don't smoke. But the emperor just pats his belly. "Nobody could fit in there. I couldn't." So he sits down to play, and he cheats. He jumps his horse right across the board. The robot moves it back. He cheats again, and the robot sweeps every piece onto the floor. The emperor loves it so much he buys it, robot and all. And from inside the box, a little voice says, "So, I live here now."

**Twist structure**
- Act: the inventor folds a lanky man into a cabinet under a robot chess player (frame 0: knees at ears, door half shut, robot on top).
- Trick explained: a "chess machine" with the town's best player hidden inside, a sliding seat to hide from the open doors, magnets to read the moves.
- Threat: the court scientist opens every door (all gears), then sniffs the tell: smoke curling from the robot's hat.
- Saved by: the fat emperor's own logic: "Nobody could fit in there. I couldn't."
- Reversal: the emperor cheats the cheat and is swept off the board, then buys the machine "robot and all", so the buddy is sold with it. The buddy's last line loops to frame 0 (folded in the box).

**Hero rig: the cabinet** (steps): 1. a maple cabinet on brass casters, three front doors and a long drawer; 2. a wall of brass gears and drums fitted behind the left door (all show, it hides empty space); 3. a seat on rails that rolls left and right as each door opens; 4. the board on top: pieces with magnets in their bases, and under each square a little iron disc on a thread that jumps up (MACRO); 5. a lever arm from the operator's peg-board up to the robot's hand; 6. the candle, with a tin chimney up the robot's body to its hat; 7. camera inside the hero object: the buddy's face lit from below by the candle, discs bobbing overhead as the emperor's moves land.
**Places (one palace):** the inventor's workshop in the palace cellar; the great hall with the board under a chandelier; inside the cabinet; the gallery where courtiers watch.
**Lights:** warm CANDLE (workshop, inside the box), cool silver WINDOW DAYLIGHT (the scientist's inspection), gold CHANDELIER EVENING (the game and the sale).
**Cast (gag each):** the Inventor (schemer, standard; tidy wig, sly half-smile): polishes the robot's nose with his sleeve whenever anyone looks too close. The Buddy inside (thin; long limbs folded like a deckchair): pushes a pawn up through a slot when nobody is looking, by mistake, then pulls it back. The Emperor (fat; huge hat, endless cape): his cape keeps catching on things. The Scientist (standard; spectacles on a stick): sniffs everything, the robot, the candle, the emperor.
Design flag: the real figure was dressed as an Orientalist "Turk". Ours must be a plain wooden automaton (robe, tall hat), no ethnic costume.
**Real vs legend:** a chess-playing "automaton" was built in 1770 for an empress's court and toured Europe and America for decades (HIGH); a strong player hid inside, slid on a moving seat as the doors were opened one by one, and followed moves through magnets and hanging discs under the board (HIGH, explained in print in the 1800s); it worked by candlelight, smoke vented up through the figure (MEDIUM). The builder promised the empress a wonder and came back in about six months (MEDIUM). An emperor played it in 1809, made illegal moves, the figure corrected them and finally swept the pieces off (MEDIUM, versions differ). It was bought in 1811 by the emperor's stepson, not the emperor (MEDIUM-HIGH). Merging two rulers into one, "every last coin", the smoke as the scientist's find, and "robot and all": DRAMATIZED. Sources: Tom Standage, *The Turk* (2002); Gerald M. Levitt, *The Turk, Chess Automaton* (2000); Robert Willis, *An Attempt to Analyse the Automaton Chess Player* (1821); Karl Gottlieb von Windisch, *Inanimate Reason* (1784); Silas Weir Mitchell, *Chess Monthly* (1857); E. A. Poe, "Maelzel's Chess-Player" (1836).

## PITCH 3: "The Giant Behind the Barn"
Kind: forged ancient find shown for money. Era/place: an American farm and a big city, 1868-69, told as a fable.

**Draft (229 words):**
He beat a ten-foot stone man with a mallet full of sewing needles. His cousin holds the lamp. "So, we're giving a rock goosebumps." You see, it's called a petrified giant, a man so old he turned to stone. They'll bury it behind the barn for a year, then dig up a giant from the old stories. It cost every dollar he has, all 2,600. They paint it with acid till it looks a thousand years old, sneak it in under a load of hay, and bury it. A year later, two men digging a well hit a stone foot, and by Sunday, people are paying fifty cents a look. Then a rich banker rolls up in a tall silk hat and steps straight into the hole. But he's brought a professor, and the professor isn't fooled. He scrubs the giant's arm and finds fresh chisel marks. This thing was carved last year. But the banker points at the crowd. "Three thousand people don't stand in a cow field for a fake." The cousin just leans on his shovel. "Three thousand and one." So he buys it for thirty thousand dollars, and those two are rich. But a showman who couldn't buy it makes a plaster giant, calls the banker's the fake, and his line is twice as long. So now the banker owns the second-best fake giant in town.

**Twist structure**
- Act: a man pocks a ten-foot stone giant with a needle-studded mallet (frame 0: lamp-lit barn, cousin holding the lamp).
- Trick explained: a "petrified giant", buried a year, then "found".
- Threat: the professor scrubs the arm and finds fresh chisel marks.
- Saved by: the banker's crowd logic: "Three thousand people don't stand in a cow field for a fake" (cousin: "Three thousand and one").
- Reversal: the banker's fortune is beaten by a fake of the fake; last words "the second-best fake giant in town". Optional sting (MEDIUM, costs about 6 s): he sues, and the judge says to bring the giant in, and if it swears it's real, he wins.

**Hero object: the giant** (steps): 1. a grey stone block crated on a wagon; 2. carvers chisel a ten-foot man lying curled, one hand on his belly; 3. the needle mallet pocks "pores" all over (MACRO); 4. a sponge of acid turns him brown and old; 5. at night, under hay on the wagon, the giant's foot sticks out and the cousin shoves the hay back over it; 6. buried behind the barn, and grass grows over the spot (the year passes); 7. camera inside the hero object: from the bottom of the pit, the giant's stone face in the foreground and a ring of gawping faces at the rim; result: a tent, a hand-painted "GIANT 50 c" board, and a line round the field.
**Places:** the farm (the barn, the field with the pit, the tent and the line, the farm lane); one city street with two rival show halls facing each other (the payoff).
**Lights:** warm LANTERN NIGHT (the carving, the hay wagon), misty grey AUTUMN MORNING (the well dig), bright NOON (the crowd and the banker); the city street at gaslit dusk for the payoff.
**Cast (gag each):** the Schemer (standard; cigar, sly): taps his cigar ash into the giant's ear. The Cousin (fat; overalls, slow deadpan): leans on the shovel in every scene. The Banker (thin and tall; silk hat): steps into the pit and his hat stays floating at ground level. The Professor (standard; spectacles, wire brush): scrubs one spot until it squeaks.
Cast note: the plaster-giant showman is a fifth figure, kept to one wide shot (his poster and his line).
**Real vs legend:** a tobacconist had a ten-foot gypsum man carved, aged it and buried it on his cousin's farm, where well-diggers "found" it a year later (HIGH); the cousin charged admission (HIGH; 25 then 50 cents, MEDIUM); his cost of about 2,600 dollars (MEDIUM-HIGH); needle-faced mallet for pores and acid staining (MEDIUM-HIGH, from his later confession); a leading paleontologist called it recent and "a most decided humbug" (HIGH; he saw it AFTER the sale, so having the professor come with the buyer is DRAMATIZED); a group of businessmen bought it (price varies, about 23,000-37,500 dollars for a share, so "thirty thousand" is MEDIUM: checker picks the number); a famous showman, refused a lease, showed a plaster copy that outdrew the original in the same city (HIGH); the judge's "if he swears he's real" line (MEDIUM); the banker as one man, the 3,000 crowd and the hat gag: DRAMATIZED. Sources: Scott Tribble, *A Colossal Hoax* (2009); James Taylor Dunn, *The True, Moral and Diverting Tale of the Cardiff Giant* (1948); The Farmers' Museum, Cooperstown NY (holds the giant); Andrew D. White, *Autobiography* (1905); 1869 Syracuse and New York newspapers.
Risk: it is the closest of the three to "salting" (fake evidence planted for a rich buyer). If CB reads it as the same story, drop it.
