## What was off in the first list
The first list was mostly big famous WWII stories (spies, navies, armies). Rick's 24 scripts are mostly SMALL:
- **About 10 are an everyday mechanism told as a story** (the tree drivers crash into, the pink cell, slow-motion falls, the second draw, storm oil, rail gaps, painkillers, slot machines, random numbers, the nap with steel balls). A thing you have seen, with a hidden reason.
- **About 9 are one clever person's trick or loophole** (the mirror on the hill, the gold shotgun, the lottery bait, the scratch cards, the knight's paper, the spear and shield, the furniture burglar, the grave bell, the token window).
- **2 are dark tales** (the memory shop, the button town), and 1 is a "levels of" piece.
- **Always:** ONE person, a small place (a betting room, a mine, a road, a jail cell), a mocker, an everyday comparison for the "you see", and a last line that turns the mocker or the first frame around. Almost never a famous name or a war room.

All 10 below follow that. None of them is a film we made or an idea we pitched (checked: Broughton bridge was an old film, so it is out).

## 1. The Box the Strongest Man Couldn't Lift — 9.5
- **Frame 0:** A magician sets a tiny wooden box on the ground and dares the strongest man in the crowd to lift it.
- **Mockery:** The strongman laughs, picks it up with one finger, and the crowd cheers.
- **Escalation:** The magician waves his hands and says "now you are weak". The strongman pulls until his veins pop, and the box doesn't move an inch.
- **You see:** There's a giant magnet under the stage, switched on by a hidden helper. It's like a fridge magnet you can't pull off, only this one could hold a horse.
- **End twist:** On the last pull, the magician sends a jolt of electricity through the handle. The strongman lets go screaming and runs off, and the crowd follows him.
- **Fact risk:** LOW-MEDIUM. Robert-Houdin, 1856 in Algeria, sent by France. He wrote it up himself, so tell it as he told it.

## 2. Why Hot Milk Froze First — 9.5
- **Frame 0:** A schoolboy shoves a cup of HOT milk into the freezer while everyone else waits for theirs to cool.
- **Mockery:** The other kids: "It'll never freeze!" His teacher: "That's not physics, that's your confusion."
- **Escalation:** An hour later his is ice cream and theirs is still slush. He asks every teacher why, and the whole class laughs every time.
- **You see:** Hot milk loses water to steam and stirs itself as it cools, so there is less of it to freeze. Like a hot drink that steams down faster than you think.
- **End twist:** Years later a visiting professor asks for questions. The class laughs again. The professor goes home, tries it, and it works. Now it carries the boy's name: the Mpemba effect.
- **Fact risk:** LOW for the story (Erasto Mpemba, Tanzania, 1963; paper 1969). The physics is still argued, so say "scientists still argue why".

## 3. The Widow Who Drilled Her Kitchen Table — 9.0
- **Frame 0:** A widow drills holes in her kitchen table and sticks champagne bottles in them UPSIDE DOWN.
- **Mockery:** Her cellar master: "Madame, we drink it the other way round."
- **Escalation:** Every day she gives each bottle a little twist, for weeks. Rivals' champagne is cloudy with yeast; everyone thinks she's gone mad.
- **You see:** The twisting slides the dead yeast down into the neck. It's like tapping ketchup to the bottom of the bottle.
- **End twist:** She freezes the neck, pops the cork, and the yeast shoots out like a plug, leaving crystal-clear champagne. Her workers are sworn to secrecy, and rivals take years to work out why hers is clear.
- **Fact risk:** LOW. Barbe-Nicole Clicquot, 1816, the riddling table.

## 4. Why Ship Owners Wanted Their Ships to Sink — 9.0
- **Frame 0:** A ship owner orders one more crate on board until the deck is almost under water, and smiles.
- **Mockery:** The crew: "She'll sink before the harbour wall." Owner: "Then I'll be rich."
- **Escalation:** The ship goes down. The owner collects the insurance, buys two more old ships and overloads them too.
- **You see:** The insurance paid more than the ship and the cargo were worth. It's like breaking your old phone because the insurance buys a new one.
- **End twist:** One man paints a line on every ship's side, and a law follows: if the water is above the line, the ship doesn't sail. It's still on every ship today, the Plimsoll line.
- **Fact risk:** LOW. "Coffin ships", Samuel Plimsoll, Merchant Shipping Act 1876.

## 5. The Mirror That Fixed a Slow Elevator — 9.0
- **Frame 0:** A janitor screws a big mirror into the wall of a hotel elevator, while the owner is about to pay a fortune for a faster one.
- **Mockery:** The owner: "Guests hate how slow it is, and you're decorating?"
- **Escalation:** The complaints pile up. The engineer's quote is huge. Then the mirror goes in, and the complaint box is empty the next week. The elevator is exactly as slow as before.
- **You see:** Waiting feels long when you have nothing to do. Give people their own face, their hair and their tie, and the ride feels short. Like a long queue that flies by when you're on your phone.
- **End twist:** That's why almost every elevator still has a mirror, and the elevator was never fixed.
- **Fact risk:** MEDIUM. A popular story told about many buildings, so tell it as the story elevator people tell.

## 6. Why Surgeons Stopped Wearing White — 8.5
- **Frame 0:** A surgeon looks up from an operation and sees green ghost blood spots floating all over the white walls.
- **Mockery:** The nurses think he's lost it: "Doctor, the walls are clean."
- **Escalation:** Everywhere he looks after a long operation: green spots on the white gowns, the ceiling, the nurses' faces. He thinks he's going blind.
- **You see:** Staring at red for an hour tires your eyes' red sensors, so white looks green. Stare at a red dot, then a white wall, and try it yourself.
- **End twist:** The hospital paints the walls and dyes the gowns green, and the ghosts vanish into them. That's why scrubs are green.
- **Fact risk:** LOW-MEDIUM. The mechanism is solid; the history is about 1914 onward, so no single surgeon by name.

## 7. The Button That Does Nothing — 8.5
- **Frame 0:** A man jams the crosswalk button forty times while traffic rushes past.
- **Mockery:** A kid beside him: "Mister, it doesn't work."
- **Escalation:** He presses harder and faster. The light changes and he looks proud, as if he did it.
- **You see:** In many cities the lights have run on timers for decades and the buttons were never connected, but pressing something makes waiting feel shorter. Like pressing the lift button again.
- **End twist:** The kid presses it once, and the light changes on cue, because it was going to anyway.
- **Fact risk:** MEDIUM-LOW. New York 2004 reporting (most buttons not working); true in many cities, not all.

## 8. The Lock Even the Banker Couldn't Open — 8.5
- **Frame 0:** Robbers drag a bank manager out of bed in his nightshirt and march him to the bank at midnight.
- **Mockery:** The robbers: "Open the vault, or else."
- **Escalation:** He spins the dial with shaking hands and it won't open. They threaten him; he cries that he can't.
- **You see:** A new clock inside the vault door won't let anyone open it before 9 am, not even the man who knows the combination. Like a phone that locks itself until morning.
- **End twist:** The robbers sit and wait for 9 am, and at 9 am the first customer walks in with the town sheriff.
- **Fact risk:** MEDIUM. Time locks are real (James Sargent, 1870s, after night kidnappings of cashiers). The sheriff ending is made up for the story.

## 9. Why Barber Poles Are Red and White — 8.5
- **Frame 0:** A barber hangs his bloody bandages to dry on a pole outside his shop.
- **Mockery:** A customer: "I only wanted a haircut."
- **Escalation:** People come in for shaves, then for teeth pulled, then for blood let out of their arm. The bandages twist round the pole in the wind.
- **You see:** Barbers were also the town's surgeons. The pole was the advert, like a shop sign everyone could read.
- **End twist:** Barbers stopped doing surgery long ago, but the bloody pole never came down.
- **Fact risk:** MEDIUM. The usual explanation, with good history behind the barber-surgeons. The "drying bandages" origin is told, not proven.

## 10. The Tea Lady Who Beat a Professor — 8.0
- **Frame 0:** A lady refuses a cup of tea because the milk went in first.
- **Mockery:** The professor: "Madam, nobody can taste that."
- **Escalation:** He sets eight cups behind her back in a secret order, sure he'll embarrass her.
- **You see:** If you guess, getting all eight right is about 1 in 70, like guessing a coin flip six times in a row.
- **End twist:** She gets every cup right, and the professor turns that afternoon into the way scientists test things today.
- **Fact risk:** LOW-MEDIUM. Muriel Bristol and Ronald Fisher, 1920s. That she got them all right is reported, not recorded.
