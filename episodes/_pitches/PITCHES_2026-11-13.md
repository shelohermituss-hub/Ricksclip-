## PITCH 1: "Why Four Soldiers Carried a Tank"
Alternative titles: "The Army That Was Made of Air" (gives away a little), "How 1,100 Men Became Two Armies".
Family: 1, the laughed-at hero, ending on a last-line twist (family 2) that loops to frame 0.
Era and place: 1944-45, the farm country of northern France, then a wide river at night. Told as a fable: no unit name, no river name, no dates in the narration.

**Line 1 (13 words, action):** "Four soldiers picked up a thirty-ton tank and carried it across a field."
- FRAME 0 (sound off): dawn mist in a hedgerow pasture. Four GIs in olive drab carry a full-size Sherman tank at hip height, one at each corner of the hull, walking in step through wet grass. The barrel bobs a hand's width with every step (the only clue, for those who look twice). One GI has a pencil behind his ear and a paint-stained sleeve. On the farm lane behind the hedge gap, two French farmers on black bicycles have stopped dead, one foot down. The tank is olive with a white star and has a chalked number on the hull front. It is readable at 360 px: a tank, four men, no crane.
- Reason hidden: the tank is a rubber balloon. The narration never says "rubber" or "balloon" until the "You see" (Rick's "the thing is described, not named").

**Setup (one exact number):** "They're painters and sign makers from art schools, all 1,100 of them, and their job is to look like thirty thousand."
(1,100 men is HIGH. "Thirty thousand", two divisions, is MEDIUM-HIGH; the checker picks the safe wording.)

**The rule that makes the clock:** the enemy must SEE them, from a plane by day and by ear at night, and never from closer than a field away. The break: the two bicycles that come through the hedge.

**Mocker:** the Tank Boss, the commander of a real tank crew, in the big close-up, cigar in his teeth, leaning over the painter's sketchbook: **"Are we fighting them, or throwing them a party?"** (8 words; it lands on the hissing compressor and the swelling shape). DRAMATIZED, not a historical quote. The hero answers with one slow blink, keeps sketching, and never explains.

**Escalation in threes (each step weirder, the mocker cockier, one comic aside):**
1. Day: a field full of tanks appears in an afternoon, and the Painter throws the camouflage nets on badly, ON PURPOSE (MEDIUM), so a plane can spot them.
2. Dusk: a bulldozer drags a tank's tread marks through the mud up to each tank (MEDIUM), and the Tank Boss laughs: they are faking footprints.
3. Night: a half-track with loudspeakers as big as bathtubs plays recorded tank engines and clanking chains across the valley (HIGH), and actors in the village café talk loudly about the wrong army (HIGH). Comic aside: the Sound Man drops the needle on the wrong record for one beat, a dance band, and snatches it off.

**"You see" (late, about 75 %, explains the trick through something the viewer has done):** "You see, it's the pillows-under-the-blanket trick. You stuff your bed, and whoever looks in the door walks right past." Staged in the story's own world: a GI in the tent bunks stuffs his bedroll with a pack and a helmet under the blanket, and the sergeant's flashlight passes over it.

**Twist and payoff (shown, landing on the mocker):** at the big river crossing, the fake army sits out in the open while every enemy gun in the valley pounds the rubber tanks miles away (MEDIUM), and the Tank Boss's REAL tank rolls across a pontoon bridge upstream in the dark without one shell near it. He looks back at the far flashes over the fake tanks and slowly takes the cigar out of his mouth.
**Last line (the biggest reveal, loops to frame 0):** "...so when two farmers on bikes see four men carry a tank, he just shrugs and says, 'Americans are very strong.'" Hard loop: the last frame is the Painter's deadpan shrug at the hedge gap with the tank on four shoulders, which is frame 0 again. (The cyclist moment happened in France, BEFORE the river. The ending is told as the answer to frame 0, so no false order is claimed. The checker should confirm the wording "Americans are very strong" or give a safe paraphrase.)
Ending per CB 6b: the hero's punchline face (the Painter's calm shrug, one brow up) in front of the result (the carried tank, the gawping cyclists).

The enemy is never shown as people, only as distant gun flashes.

- Visual payoff: a field of a hundred inflating barrels at dusk, then shellfire on rubber tanks while the real one crosses in the dark. The loop gives the cyclists' faces.
- Comedy: art students in a war (contrast comedy: the calm painter beside the cigar-chewing boss), the party-horn barrel, the wrong record, the sinking lean, and the "Americans are very strong" button.

## PITCH 2: "Why He Sailed a Frozen Pond to a Hot Island"
Alternative titles: "The Man Who Gave Away Ice", "How One Man Taught a Hot Island to Want Ice".
Family: 1, the laughed-at hero, with a family-2 trick in the last third (he gives it away for free).
Era and place: 1806-1830s, a pond and wharf near Boston in winter, then a Caribbean port in blazing sun. Told as a fable: "a merchant's son", "a hot island", no names or years.

**Line 1 (14 words, action):** "He sawed a frozen pond into blocks and sailed them to a hot island."
- FRAME 0 (sound off): a palm-lined stone wharf at noon, heat shimmer over the water. The Ice Man, still in his wool greatcoat and fur hat, hauls a block of clear blue-white ice as big as a sea chest up out of a ship's hold with iron ice tongs. Sawdust falls off it in a cloud, and meltwater drips and steams on the hot stones. Behind him the brig's hatch is open on a hold packed with golden sawdust. Dockworkers and a fruit seller with a basket of mangoes stare. The readable things: ice, a man in a winter coat, palm trees.
- Reason hidden: why ice, why there, and what the sawdust is for.

**Setup (one exact number):** "Back home the pond freezes a foot thick, and he packs a hundred and thirty tons of it into one ship." (130 tons is the usual figure. The newspaper said 80. The checker picks one; flagged.)

**The rule that makes the clock:** ice melts, so every hour in the sun counts. A clock on the wall and a puddle growing under the block measure it in each beat.

**Mocker:** the Wharf Merchant, a fat man in a top hat with the town newspaper open, reads it aloud into the Ice Man's face as the ship casts off: **"We hope this will not prove a slippery speculation."** (9 words). This is the REAL printed joke (Boston Gazette, February 1806; the exact wording needs checking). The hero tips his fur hat and says nothing.

**Escalation in threes:**
1. The first voyage: the ice arrives almost whole (MEDIUM), but nobody on the island has ever held ice. It burns their fingers with cold, a lady drops it with a shriek, and it melts in the sun while he watches. He goes home broke (MEDIUM: he lost money; HIGH: later he went to jail for debt).
2. He fixes the ice: a horse-drawn plough with iron teeth scores the frozen pond into a perfect chessboard, and every block comes out the same size, so they stack without a gap (HIGH, a later invention by his supplier, shown here as "he gets better").
3. He fixes the hold: double plank walls with sawdust packed between, waste sawdust from the lumber mills, which nobody wanted either (HIGH in general).
Comic aside: the Merchant's newspaper headline is pinned in the Ice Man's ice house, and he uses it as a coaster.

**"You see" (late, about 70 %, an everyday thing the viewer has done):** "You see, a snowman lasts all week, but a snowball in your mitten is gone by lunch. Big and packed tight, ice hardly melts, and sawdust keeps the warm air off like a blanket." Staged in the story world: Boston kids, a fat snowman in the ice-house yard beside a melting snowball in a small mitten.

**Twist and payoff (the trick, shown, landing on the mocker):** "Then he does the weirdest thing yet: he gives it away." Free ice to every bar and café on the island, as long as they sell cold drinks for the same price as warm ones (MEDIUM-HIGH, from his diary as told by historians). A summer later nobody on the island will drink anything warm, and he starts charging. Final beat: the Wharf Merchant, red and sweating on a business trip to the island, orders a drink "with ice". The bartender drops in a block, and the Ice Man, at the next table in his fur hat, slides the newspaper coaster under the glass. The merchant on the island is DRAMATIZED. The last frame is the hero's smug face over his cold glass in front of a packed, clinking café, with the ship unloading behind him, and it loops to frame 0 (the block coming out of the hold).
Optional extra step (costs about 4 s): "Soon his ice survives four months at sea to the other side of the world" (the 1833 Calcutta voyage, HIGH).

| 1 | the horse-drawn ice plough scores the pond into a chessboard; ice chips spray and the horse's breath steams |
| 2 | men saw along the grooves with long ice saws and pole the blocks down an open-water channel (MACRO: the saw teeth, the blue-green inside of a fresh block) |
| 3 | the blocks slide up a wooden ramp into the ice house: double plank walls, sawdust between, a peaked roof with a vent |
| 4 | the ship's hold is lined with planks; blocks are stacked tight with no gaps, and sawdust is poured in a golden waterfall over the top |
| 5 (camera inside the hero object) | inside the dark hold among the stacked blocks, lantern light glowing through the ice and sawdust drifting down |
| 6 | the hatch is nailed shut, and the ship sails out past the snowy point |
| result | at the island: the tongs, the steaming block, glasses clinking in the café |

**Places (7, one world tied by the ship and the ice tongs):**
1. The frozen pond with the ice house (winter morning).
2. The snowy wharf and the Merchant's counting house (winter dusk).
3. The ship's hold (lantern).
4. The open sea, heading south (hot noon, sails full).
5. The island wharf under the green mountain (blazing noon, frame 0).
6. The island café with louvred shutters and ceiling fans worked by a pull cord (warm lantern evening).
7. The Boston kids' snow yard (the "You see" analogy).
**Lights (story clock, three kinds):** cold BLUE WINTER MORNING and dusk (snow, steam breath), WHITE-HOT TROPICAL NOON (hard short shadows, heat shimmer, ice glints), warm LANTERN EVENING in the café.
Signature warm light: the ship's brass hold lantern, the warm note inside every cold scene, and it glows through the ice.

**Cast:**
- **The Ice Man** (hero, standard): a young merchant's son in a wool greatcoat and fur hat, which he NEVER takes off, even in the tropics (his signature detail and running gag). Calm, stubborn, a sly half-smile, always has iron ice tongs. Gag: he checks the ice the way a doctor takes a pulse, palm flat on the block, eyes closed.
- **The Wharf Merchant** (mocker, fat): top hat, waistcoat buttons straining, newspaper always in hand. Loud and smug, and he laughs with his belly. Gag: he slips on the meltwater puddle at his own "slippery" line, catches himself on a bollard, and pretends it was a bow.
- **The Café Owner** (thin, island side): sceptical, arms crossed at the first free block, then the first to beg for more. Gag: he holds the first piece of ice at arm's length with sugar tongs like a live crab.
- Background: the plough horse, frosty whiskers.
- Care note: the island scenes are set in a colonial port of 1806, a slave society. The cast and crowd must be drawn with dignity and with no caricature of island people. The joke is always on the Merchant and on the ice, never on the islanders. CB should decide whether that setting is welcome before choosing this pitch.

**Teams and banners (6b):** cold Boston in navy, slate and frost white, with "GAZETTE" mastheads and "ICE" painted on the ice-house door, against the warm island in coral, turquoise and palm green, with the café's chalkboard "COLD DRINKS - SAME PRICE".
**Words in the world:** "MARTINIQUE" (crate stencil; or "HOT ISLAND" if CB wants no place names), "FAVORITE" (the brig's stern; the real name, to check), "GAZETTE", "ICE", "COLD DRINKS - SAME PRICE", "FREE" (a card on the ice barrel).

## PITCH 3: "Why a Cloth Seller Stared Into Pepper Water"
Alternative titles: "The Shopkeeper Who Found Animals in a Raindrop", "Why a Shopkeeper Never Trusted a Raindrop".
Family: 1, the laughed-at hero (the learned men across the sea doubt him, and his neighbour laughs), ending on a payoff that lands right inside the mocker's mouth.
Era and place: the 1670s, a canal town in Holland (Delft, never named), with one scene in a learned society's hall across the sea.

**Line 1 (13 words, action):** "He stared into a bowl of pepper water through a tiny brass plate."
- FRAME 0 (sound off): a tall leaded window with grey-white north light. The Cloth Seller in his long brown coat and white collar holds a brass plate the size of a playing card right against his eye, one eye squeezed shut, tongue at the corner of his mouth. In his other hand a glass tube drips one drop of murky water onto the pin in front of the lens. A blue-and-white bowl of peppercorns soaking in water sits big in the foreground, with a stoneware jar painted "PEPPER". Behind him are bolts of cloth. Readable at phone size: the man, the glinting plate at his eye, the pepper bowl. (The plate is small, so it is held up into the window light as a bright glint against his dark coat, and frame 0 is a medium-close shot. Phone check at storyboard.)
- Reason hidden: he wanted to see why pepper burns the tongue (he expected tiny spikes), and what he finds is alive.

**Setup (one exact number):** "He sells cloth, and to count threads he makes a glass bead smaller than a lentil that makes things two hundred times bigger." (His best surviving lens is about 266 times. "Two hundred" is the safe figure; flagged.)

**The rule that makes the clock:** nobody may see how he makes his lenses. He keeps his method secret all his life (HIGH): a curtain, a locked drawer, and a "back later" card on the workshop door. That secret is why the learned men cannot believe him.

**Mocker:** the Brewer from next door, a fat man in a leather apron who smells of malt, peers over the half-door with a tankard: **"Next you'll tell me there are fish in my beer."** (10 words). DRAMATIZED. The hero blinks once, offers him the brass plate, and the Brewer waves it away.

**Escalation in threes:**
1. The pepper water: tiny animals swimming, spinning and bumping (HIGH, his 1676 letter).
2. The rain barrel: fresh rainwater from a new tub has them too after a few days (HIGH, 1675). The Brewer stops drinking water, which is fine by him, he drinks beer.
3. The learned men across the sea read his letter and do not believe it, so he sends them signed statements from eight witnesses who looked through his lens, among them the town's English minister and a notary (HIGH that he sent testimonials; the exact count of eight is MEDIUM). Then their own instrument maker builds a lens and sees the animals too (HIGH, 1677). Comic aside: the nervous minister signs the "WE SAW THEM" paper with a shaking quill, then quietly pours his water jug out of the window.

**"You see" (late, about 70 %, an everyday thing):** "You see, it's like a drop of water on your table that makes the wood grain look fat. The smaller and rounder the glass, the bigger the world gets." Staged in the shop: a drop of spilt water on a bolt of linen swells the threads under it, and the Cloth Seller grins at it.

**Twist and payoff (shown, landing on the mocker):** the Cloth Seller cleans his own teeth with salt and a cloth every morning (HIGH), and he still finds a few animals in the white stuff on them (HIGH, his 1683 letter). Then he scrapes the Brewer's teeth. The Brewer, who has never cleaned them in his life, looks through the lens: we go INSIDE the lens to a glowing gold disc of water where thousands of wriggling, spinning, corkscrewing creatures crowd the frame, more than there are people in the whole country (his own comparison, HIGH as a paraphrase). The Brewer's face goes from smirk to green. He drops the tankard and scrubs his teeth with the salt and the cloth, and his own beer foam drips off his chin. The last frame is the Cloth Seller's smug half-smile, arms crossed, in front of the scrubbing Brewer. The loop goes to frame 0 (the brass plate at his eye). The link "the old man who never cleaned his teeth = the Brewer" is DRAMATIZED: his real samples came from unnamed people, among them an old man who had never cleaned his teeth.

**HERO OBJECT: the single-lens microscope, built on screen (one line per step):**
| step | picture |
|---|---|
| 1 | at a foot-pedal grinding wheel by candlelight, he grinds a glass bead smaller than a lentil; grit and water spray (the method is debated: grinding is shown, and a blown bead is possible; flagged) |
| 2 | he hammers two thin brass plates flat on an anvil, and the hammer marks catch the light |
| 3 | he drills a pin-sized hole in each plate and sets the bead between them; four rivets are hammered in (MACRO) |
| 4 | a long screw goes through a bracket on the back and carries a sharp pin that holds the sample in front of the lens |
| 5 | a second screw moves the pin up and down to focus, and he turns it with a fingertip, tongue out |
| 6 (camera inside the hero object) | through the lens: the blur sharpens into a glowing disc full of swimmers |
| result | a drawer of dozens of finished plates, each with its own sample already pinned (he kept many, HIGH), and finally the Brewer's tooth scrapings |

**Places (6-7, one canal town; the same tall church spire and the same windmill from every side):**
1. The cloth shop at street level: bolts of wool and linen, a counting glass on a cord, the half-door to the canal.
2. The upstairs workshop behind its curtain: the grinding wheel, the anvil, the brass offcuts, the candle.
3. The yard with the new rain tub, then a canal-side street in the rain.
4. The micro-world inside the drop, a place of its own with its own light: a glowing gold-green disc, swimmers lit from behind.
5. The Brewer's tavern next door: copper kettle, barrels, the hop-pole sign.
6. The learned society's hall across the sea: a long table, candles, periwigs, the letter with its wax seal.
7. The witnesses lining up on the shop's stairs.
**Lights (story clock, three kinds):** cool grey-white NORTH WINDOW DAYLIGHT (shop and frame 0), blue-grey RAIN DUSK on the canal, warm CANDLE NIGHT in the workshop and the society hall, and a bright clear MORNING for the payoff.
Signature warm light: his grinding-bench candle, which also lights the micro-world disc from behind (the warm glow through the lens in every scene).

**Cast:**
- **The Cloth Seller** (hero, standard): a long brown coat, plain white collar and shoulder-length hair under a soft cap, a counting glass on a cord round his neck (his signature detail). Patient, secretive, quietly proud. Gag: whenever anyone comes near the workshop curtain he steps in front of it with a wide innocent smile.
- **The Brewer** (mocker, fat): a leather apron, rolled sleeves, a tankard always in hand, and yellow teeth when he laughs. Loud, sure, never washes. Gag: he wipes the beer foam off his lip with his sleeve, then wipes the sleeve on his tooth after the reveal.
