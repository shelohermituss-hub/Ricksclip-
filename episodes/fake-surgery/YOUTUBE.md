## Description
A surgeon cuts three tiny holes in a man's knee, splashes some water around, and sticks on three plasters. 🩹 That's it. He never goes inside the knee. The nurse leans in and whispers: "Doctor, you forgot the operation."

Back in the 1990s in Houston, surgeons scraped and rinsed sore knees all day long. Then a colleague asked a nasty question: what if it's all in their heads? So 180 sore knees were split into three groups: scraped, rinsed, and… the trick.

Two years later the man from the table is climbing stairs again. And the real-surgery groups? No better at all.

📜 Science note: the core is real. In a trial at the Houston VA Medical Center, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in July 2002, 180 veterans with knee osteoarthritis were randomly given arthroscopic debridement, a lavage (rinse), or a pretend operation: small skin cuts, no instrument in the joint, a recorded arthroscopy playing, the leg moved and saline splashed so it sounded real. Every patient had signed a form saying they might get a pretend surgery. After two years all three groups had improved, and the pretend group did as well as the real ones on pain and function. In 2004 Medicare stopped paying for these operations for arthritis knee pain, and later trials (Canada 2008, Finland 2013) found similar results. This is about arthroscopy for arthritis knee pain only; it is not medical advice. The surgeon, the nurse, the veteran and the kid in the waiting room are our cartoon stand-ins, not real people.
