# FACTS: "Why Fire Trucks Turned Yellow"

Checked 2026-11-20, web sources only (search summaries; the paper's full text was not opened). Confidence H / M / L.

| # | Claim | Verdict | Conf | Source | Safe wording |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | In dim light the eye loses red first; yellow-green stays brightest (Purkinje shift) | TRUE | H | Wikipedia "Purkinje effect"; Hanover College ISLE simulation; Florida Tech "Psychology Science Minute" | "when the light gets dim, your eyes lose red first" |
| 2 | Lime-yellow fire trucks appeared across the US in the 1970s | TRUE | H | Time magazine (1970s article on lime-yellow engines); APA "Fire engine colour safety" | "in the nineteen-seventies" |
| 3 | The man behind it was an eye doctor who was also a volunteer fireman | TRUE | M | Time: Stephen Solomon, optometrist, Port Jervis N.Y. volunteer fire department | No real name said, no likeness. He did research; that HE sprayed a truck is our cartoon picture (11e rule 4) |
| 4 | Dallas ran red and lime-yellow pumpers side by side; four years of records | TRUE | H | Solomon and King, "Influence of Color on Fire Vehicle Accidents", Journal of Safety Research 26(1), 1995 (TRID 415682; Fire Engineering); period 1 Oct 1984 - 30 Sep 1988 | "for four years" |
| 5 | Red trucks: up to three times the risk of visibility-related crashes with other vehicles | TRUE | H | same (abstract: "as much as three times greater") | "up to three times as often"; never a flat "three times" |
| 6 | Dallas later went back to red | TRUE | M | Fire Engineering / FireRescue1 summaries | "Dallas went back to red anyway" (not "most cities") |
| 7 | Why back to red: people recognise a red truck as a fire truck | PARTLY | M | 2009 FEMA/USFA review: recognising the vehicle matters more than paint colour | Said as the chief's feeling ("a fire truck just has to be red"), not as science |
| 8 | Firemen disliked the yellow trucks | LIKELY | M | APA article, FireRescue1 (tradition) | "plenty of firemen hated them" |

Must avoid: "twice the crashes" as a Dallas number (that figure is from a different nine-city study); any claim that yellow trucks are proven safer everywhere; any injury on screen (kid-safe bump only).
Correction to the pitch: the 1995 paper is in the Journal of Safety Research, not the optometry journal.
